Readings


The general journal provides a chronological list of all of the transactions for a business. A
chronological list on its own, however, is of limited use. Classifying and summarising data
improves their usefulness for decision making. For example, a business’s general journal could
contain hundreds of purchases, cash receipts and cash payments transactions in any given month.
If a sales manager wants to know the total sales revenue for any particular month, this information
cannot be easily obtained from the general journal, but it can be obtained from the sales revenue
account in the ledger. The ledger accumulates all the information about changes in specific
account balances such as sales revenue, cash, and wages expense.

The entire group of accounts maintained by a business is referred to collectively as the general
ledger. A general ledger contains all the assets, liabilities and equity accounts, as shown below.

T-format vs Column format

Accounts are found in the General Ledger. Accounts can be presented in the T format or the
columnar format. Once a transaction is analysed, entries can be made to the related accounts and
the double entry rule followed.
Accounts in T format are similar to the capital letter T with the name of an account shown at the top of
the T. The left hand side of the T account is called the debit side and includes column headings of the
date, particulars and debit amount. The right hand side of the T account is called the credit side and
also includes column headings of the date, particulars and credit amount. The particulars can also be
called the description column and is used to record the name of the corresponding account affected.

Accounts in the columnar format show the name of the account at the top and include the column headings of

The main differences between the T and columnar formats are the one date and particular columns, the debit

Posting in T-format

The procedure of transferring journal entries to ledger accounts is called posting. This phase of the recording
process accumulates the effects of journalised transactions in the individual asset, liability, equity, revenue and
expense accounts.

Each account has a name. To record a transaction in an account:
•
the same date is recorded in both accounts because they refer to the same transaction
that occurs on 1 January.
•
 In the particulars column, write the name of the corresponding account so the two
accounts cross refer to one another. The amount is written in the debit and credit column
accordingly.

For example, to record the transaction:
“On 1 January, the owner contributes $10,000 cash as capital to start their business”.

In the Cash at Bank Account below, the date is Jan 1, the corresponding account name is Capital,
and the amount is 10,000 on the debit side.

In the Capital Account below, the date is Jan 1, the corresponding account name is Cash at Bank,
and the amount is 10,000 on the credit side.

Balancing in T-format

(Note: b/d = brought down, c/d = carried down)

Once recording is complete, a trial balance is prepared. To facilitate this process, the balance of each account
in the general ledger must be calculated. The process of balancing a T account is illustrated using the cash
account below. Balancing involves the following main steps:
•
For each account, add each side. Generally, the normal account balance side will be larger - (Debit side
for Assets, Expenses & Drawings, Credit side for Liabilities, Owner’s Equity & Revenue)
•
The closing balance for the account can now be determined by subtracting the amounts listed on the
lesser side from the total. The words ‘balance c/d or closing balance’ and the amount calculated in this
step are recorded on the lesser side (generally the side opposite to the normal balance side).
•
The date (the first day of the next month), the words ‘balance b/d or opening balance’ and the balance
calculated in step 3 are written below the ruled-off lines on the greater account balance side (usually the
normal balance side).

Once these four steps have been completed, the account has been balanced. It is very important to remember
that the closing balance is only a balancing item, i.e. the amount needed to make the debit side equal the credit
side and have the totals in balance. It is not the correct account balance because it is on the wrong side. The
opening balance will be the correct account balance for the account.

In the Cash account above, the debit side of the cash account is the greater side and the normal account balance
of the cash account. Adding the debit side reveals a total of $55000. This amount is written between the ruled
lines on both the debit and credit side of the account. All the amounts listed on the credit side of the account are
then subtracted from the total to reveal a closing balance/balance c/d of $39000. This means that the debit side
of the account is $39000 greater than the credit side. This amount is recorded on the debit side of the cash
account on 1 November as the opening balance/balance b/d. The November transactions can then be recorded
in the account.

Balancing a T format account -Illustration
The balance of an account is essentially the difference between the debit side and the credit side of
the account. If the debit side adds up to be greater than the credit side, the balance is called a debit
balance. If, on the other hand, the credit side adds up to be greater than the debit side, the difference
is a credit balance. The T format account requires periodic balancing. This is a disadvantage.

To balance an account, the following steps should be followed. A Cash at Bank Account is used
as an example to illustrate how to balance an account.

Find the total of the debit side. The debit total is $16,000 (15,000 + 1,000 = 16,000).
Find the total of the credit side. The credit total is $3,120 (500 + 2,000 + 60
+ 560 = 3,120).
Find the difference by subtracting the smaller total from the bigger total. The difference is
$12,880 (16,000 – 3,120 = 12,880).
Place the difference under the side that has a smaller total. In this example,
$12,880 is placed under the credit side. This difference is noted as Balance to be
carried down or simply Balance c/d for short.
Then total up the debit side, write down the total ($16,000) with a double underline.
Then total up the credit side, write down the total ($16,000) with a double underline.
•
At the start of the next period, the balance is brought down to the opposite side and is
noted as Balance b/d for short.

In this example, $12,880 is called a debit balance because the debit amount is greater than the credit

The balance at the start of a month is also known as the opening balance/balance b/d of an
account. In this example, $12, 880 is the opening balance for the month of April.

The balance at the end of a month is also known as the closing balance/balance b/d of an
account. In this example, the closing balance of the account for March is $12, 880.

Accounting Conventions
Development of Accounting
The double entry system was first outlined in the late 1400’s by Pacioli, a Franciscan
monk and mathematician. Accounting was used by merchants to record their
expenditure and to show 'what they had left’ (ie stewardship).

Over the centuries, the emphasis of accounting has changed from asking 'what
happened?' (collecting, recording and summarising) to 'what should we do?'
(analysing and reporting). The preparation of financial information for decision making
is now the key focus.

The nature of accounting has changed to meet the needs of different types and sizes
of businesses. Businesses can vary greatly in size - from sole traders to large
companies. They can be profit-making enterprises or not-for-profit organisations such
as clubs, charities or societies. Any organisation where money changes hands for
goods or services can be regarded as a business.

Businesses differ in their types of activities:

Service businesses provide a service, eg a motel, doctor, or education
providers.

Trading or merchandising businesses buy and sell goods, eg an
importer of shoes.

Manufacturing businesses purchase raw materials and produce products
to sell, eg shoe manufacturing.

Farming businesses use the land to produce food or raw materials eg
wool.

As the nature of commerce has changed, the demands on the accounting profession
have increased. Today the accounting system must cope with complicated and
changing tax laws and high-pressure business activity. It must assist in decision
making for all users of accounting information, from individuals and clubs to huge
multinational corporations and governments.

Sources of accounting regulation
Historically, the accounting system is based on a number of conventions and
doctrines. They provide a foundation for the accounting process. However, the
complexity of business activity found the conventions to be inadequate for the needs
of modern accounting. The need to provide more direction has given rise to the
development and implementation of Australian Accounting Standards and also
Statements of Accounting Concepts.

Conventions are practices or rules and accepted customs that have developed over
time in the accounting profession. They are practices concerned with preparing
and presenting financial information that is assumed by all accountants including
students. Some of these are listed below.

Monetary convention
Monetary measurement means that all information must be described in dollar terms.
Limitations:
Inflation makes comparisons difficult because the purchasing power of the dollar
changes from year to year. The changing value of the dollar means, for example, that
a profit of $10,000 in 1970 cannot be directly compared with a profit of $20,000 in
2020.

Some things cannot be measured in monetary terms, eg good management and
excellent employer-employee relations. These contribute greatly to the success of a
business but cannot be measured easily in dollar terms.

Accounting entity convention
There needs to be a clear boundary (from an accounting point of view) separating the
financial affairs of the accounting entity from other activities. For example, the
personal financial affairs of the owner of a business are kept separate from the affairs
of the business itself. All business transactions are recorded from the point of view of
the business, not the owner.

For example, John is the owner of the business Lighting Warehouse. The business has
cash $10,000, stock $16,000 and a bank loan $30,000. The assets of the business
total $56,000. John’s wedding ring worth $2,000 is not an asset of the business. It
is not included in the calculation of the business assets.

Continuity/Going concern convention
This convention assumes that a business is set up to continue indefinitely. Therefore
the assets other than stock for sale are assumed to be used in the operation rather
than sold for profit.

Historical cost convention
Assets are valued at the original purchase price and recorded at the actual amount.
Therefore, it is acceptable to use historical costs in the Statement of Financial
Position.

Accounting period convention
The life of a business is split into regular and equal intervals or periods, usually one
year or annually. This allows the business activities and performance to be measured.
Eg the accounting period could be from 1 July 2020 to 30 June 2021 (also known as
the financial year in Australia) or 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2020. The accounting
reports are prepared for the specific period to enable an assessment of the results from
trading, and a meaningful comparison with expected or past results.

Double Entry convention or Dual aspect convention
This convention states that each transaction has a minimum of two aspects and thus
a minimum of two accounts are affected. For example the introduction of cash as
capital will increase the asset (cash) and also increase the owner’s equity (capital).
Thus the purchase of a car for cash would result in an increase in the asset (car) and
a decrease in another asset (cash). Any of the five elements could be affected.
Applying the double entry convention helps to maintain the equality of the accounting
equation.

Consistency Convention
This is a principle that the same accounting principles should be used for
preparing financial statements over a number of time periods. This enables the
management to draw important conclusions regarding the working of the concern over
a longer period. It allows a comparison in the performance of different periods. If
different accounting procedures and processes are used for preparing financial
statements of different years then the results will not be comparable because these
will be based on different postulates.

Accrual Convention
An accounting system that tries to match the recognition of revenues earned with
the expenses incurred in generating those revenues. It ignores the timing of the cash
flows associated with revenues and expenses.

Cash Vs Accrual Accounting
1. Cash accounting – cash accounting is concerned with recording accounting
transactions at the time only when cash is received and cash is paid. As an
example this means recording revenue only when cash is received and
expenses when cash is paid. Most small businesses use this method.
2. Accrual accounting – accrual accounting is concerned with recording accounting
transactions at the time they take place irrespective of the exchange of cash. This
means that in an accounting period recording revenues as earned and expenses as
incurred regardless of cash being paid or received. This method uses the concept
of matching revenues and expenses in an accounting period as to when and how
to record transactions. Thus a sale of goods on credit gives rise to revenue and
accounts receivable and a purchase of goods on credit gives rise to purchases and
accounts payable in the accounting period. This method provides a more
meaningful profit/loss result and most medium/large businesses use this method.
 For most merchandising businesses, using the accrual basis of accounting means
recording revenues when goods are sold rather than when the cash is received.
For entities that generate revenue by providing services, the accrual basis of
accounting is typically applied by recording revenue when the service is performed
rather than when the customer or client pays for the service. If services are part
of a long-term contract, the revenue may need to be recognised progressively
over several periods. The accounting reports prepared using the accrual-based
system better reflect the true position and performance of the business for the
period covered by the accounting period.
The matching of Revenues and Expenses for the defined accounting period is an important
part of profit/loss measurement and the subsequent preparation of the Income Statement
and Balance Sheet. A business could use either of 2 accounting methods in order to record
transactions and determine their profit; cash accounting or accrual accounting.

Conservatism
Conservatism, or prudence, is a principle that dictates accountants should recognize potential
losses when they are possible, but only record potential gains when they are certain. This
practice ensures that financial statements are not misleading by overstating assets or income.

The revenue recognition principle
The revenue recognition principle is an accounting concept under accrual
accounting that states revenue should be recorded only when it has been earned and
is realized or realizable, regardless of when payment is received.

Accounting Equation convention
A basic accounting equation is used to implicitly express that Assets (A) equals Liabilities
(L) plus Owner’s Equity (OE). The accounting equation can thus be expressed as:
A = L + OE
Or
A – L = OE (this is also known as Net Assets)
The basic accounting equation can also be expanded to include Revenues (R) and
Expenses (E).

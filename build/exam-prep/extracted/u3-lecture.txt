ELEMENTS OF ACCOUNTING

=== PAGE 4 ===
1. Elements of Accounting - Assets (A)
•
Items of value a business owns or owed to a business.
•
Assets provide a future economic benefit to the business
•
Examples include cash, furniture and motor vehicles.
•
The Assets can be further subdivided into current assets and non-
current assets.

=== PAGE 5 ===
Current vs Non-Current Assets
Current
•
Assets expected to be consumed or
used up within one accounting
period.
•
Usually < 12 months.
•
Easily converted to cas E.g. Cash,
Stock, Accounts Receivable/Debtors.
Non-Current
• Assets used by the business for more
than one accounting period.
• Usually > 12 months.
• Not easily converted into cash. E.g.
Motor Vehicle, Equipment, Furniture

=== PAGE 6 ===
2. Elements of Accounting: Liabilities (L)
•
What the business owes to persons or other businesses
other than the owners.
•
Examples include loans and accounts payable/creditors
•
Liabilities can be subdivided into current liabilities and
non-current liabilities.

=== PAGE 7 ===
Current v Non-Current Liabilities
Current
• Short term financial obligation
• Usually < 12 months
• E.g. Accounts Payable/Creditors
Non-Current
•
Long term financial obligations
•
Usually > 12 months
•
E.g. Loan (30 years)

=== PAGE 8 ===
Why classify into Current and Non-Current?
•
Required by regulations/accounting standards
•
For business planning;
➢awareness of the amount of liquid (cash) assets available
to meet debts as they fall due
➢ to finance investments and purchases
•
For clear presentation and ease of reading

=== PAGE 18 ===
Accounting terms : Account Receivable (Debtors - Customers)
•
Accounts receivable (A/R) is the term used to describe money owed to a
business by its customers for purchases made on credit.
•
Also known as trade receivables.
•
It is listed as a current asset on the balance sheet, because of the short-
term nature of standard payment terms.
•
Only purchases made by customers on credit terms are accounts receivable,
as cash payments are received immediately.
•
Suppliers extending credit to their buyers is a common feature of supply
chains, with 30-day and 60-day terms, both typical.

=== PAGE 19 ===
Accounting terms: Accounts Payable (Creditors - Suppliers)
•
Accounts payable (AP) represents the amount that a company owes to its
creditors and suppliers for purchases made on credit.
•
Also known as trade payables
•
It is a current liability in the balance sheet due to its short duration till
payment is due.
•
When a business purchases goods or services from a supplier on credit,
payment isn’t made straight away, but is due typically within 30 days or 60
days.

Inventory recording methods: Periodic Vs Perpetual
Periodic:
•
A system where inventory records are updated at
specific intervals, such as monthly or annually.
Inventory account are not updated continuously
but rather are determined by physical counts and
periodic adjustments.
•
This method involves the business recording the
purchase of Inventory into the expense account
named Purchases.
•
Purchase of inventory is recorded as:
Purchase (Expense) – Dr
       A/P or Cash ---------------Cr
Perpetual:
•
A system where inventory records are updated
continuously rather are determined by physical
counts and periodic adjustments.
•
This method involves the business recording the
purchase of Inventory into the expense account
named Purchases.
•
Purchase of inventory is recorded as:
Inventory / Stock (Asset reduces) – Dr
       A/P or Cash ---------------Cr
Cost of goods sold (Expense) ----Dr
      Inventory ---------Cr

=== PAGE 27 ===
A Chart of Accounts
•
A chart of accounts lists all the accounts in the general ledger
under the elements.
•
The account code may be in a numeric or alpha-numeric sequence.
•
Businesses in different industries will tend to have different
accounts and therefore a different chart of accounts.
•
A chart of accounts is tailored to each individual business.

Remember the Double Entry Convention?
•
Each transaction affects two or more accounts, one on the debit
side and the other on the credit side.
•
Any of the five elements could be affected.
•
 A minimum of one Debit and one Credit entry per transaction
•
 Also, Debits (Dr) = Credits (Cr)

=== PAGE 30 ===
•
Every transaction involves both a debit and a credit.
•
Each transaction impacts two or more accounts.
•
Total debits must equal total credits for each transaction.

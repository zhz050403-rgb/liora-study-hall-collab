Elements of Accounting
In order to classify and describe items exchanged in accounting transactions, five (5)
elements or groupings are required.
The 5 elements (or groupings) are:
Assets (A): items of value a business owns or owed to a business eg cash, furniture and
motor vehicle. The Assets can be subdivided into current assets and non-current assets.
•
Current assets are assets expected to be consumed or used up within one
accounting period. They can be easily converted into cash. The examples
of current assets are cash, stock and accounts receivable (debtors).
•
Non-current assets are assets that will remain with the business for
longer than one accounting period. They will be used up over many
accounting periods.
Examples are equipment, furniture, motor vehicle, goodwill and investments.
Liabilities (L): what the business owes to persons or other businesses other than the
owners eg trade creditors and loans. Liabilities can be subdivided into current liabilities
and non-current liabilities.
•
Current liabilities are amounts owed to persons or businesses that have to
be repaid within one accounting year, for example, accounts payable
(creditors) and bank overdraft.
•
Non-current liabilities are commitments which do not need to be met
within twelve months of the balance date, for example, mortgage loans.

Revenues (R) are the income of the business. Revenue can be from different
sources such as sales, rent received, interest received, commission received,
discounts received, and service fees received. The accounts that keep record of such
revenue items are found in the general ledger of a business. They include sales,
rent received, interest received, commission received, discounts received and
service revenue.

Revenue items help to generate profit for the business and subsequently increases
owner’s equity.

Expenses (E) are outgoings or costs to pay for items that enable the business to
operate to continue earning income. Some examples of expenses are cost of goods
sold, wages, rent, bank fees, telephone and advertising.

Expenses decrease profits for the business and subsequently decreases owner’s equity
Owner’s Equity (OE): what the business owes its owners or what belongs to the owners
eg capital and drawings.
•
Capital is the amount the owner has put into or contributed to the business.
•
Drawings is a withdrawal of cash or stock from the business by the owner.
•
Any profit made by the business will increase the owner’s equity in the business.
•
Any loss incurred by the business will reduce the owner’s equity in the business.

Why do you have to separate into current or non current?

•
Clear presentation
•
To comply with accounting standards and regulations
•
Required for good business planning. For example the business should be aware
of the liquid assets in order to meet short debts or fund any future purchases
and/or investments.

Accounts associated with the income statement

An income statement (or a profit and loss statement) is another important financial
report of a business. It is a statement of the financial performance of a business over
a period of time. The income statement reports on the revenues (incomes) and
expenses of a business. Because the income statement reports on the revenues and
expenses, the accounts associated with these two groups are referred to as the
income statement accounts.

In general, the difference between the revenue (R) and expenses (E) is shown as
either a profit or a loss and usually the report is presented in the vertical format.

Revenues are the income of the business. Revenue can be from different sources
such as sales, rent received, interest received, commission received, discounts
received, and service fees received. The accounts that keep record of such revenue
items are found in the general ledger of a business. They include sales, rent
received, interest received, commission received, discounts received and service
revenue.
Revenue items help to generate profit for the business and subsequently increases
owner’s equity.

Expenses are outgoings or costs to pay for items that enable the business to operate
to continue earning income. Some examples of expenses are cost of goods sold,
wages, rent, bank fees, telephone and advertising.

Expenses decrease profits for the business and subsequently decreases owner’s equity.

Accounts associated with the balance sheet

The balance sheet is an important financial report. It is a report or statement about
the financial position of a business as at a particular date. It is a detailed version of
the accounting equation. In report format, it is a listing of the assets owned by a
business, the liabilities of the business and the owner’s equity in the business.

•
Some examples of accounts in the asset element or group are cash at bank,
accounts receivable, stock, machinery, furniture, land and building, plant and
equipment and motor vehicles.
•
Some examples of accounts in the liability element or group are accounts
payable and loans.
•
Some examples of accounts in the owner’s equity element or group are capital
and drawings.

The Expanded Accounting Equation
The expanded equation can be represented as:
A + E = L + OE + R
or
A = L + OE + (R – E)
(R - E) represents the calculation of profit or loss.
If R > E, the business makes a profit. Profit is a reward for risk taking and is added to
the owner’s equity as it belongs to the owners.
If R < E, the business makes a loss. Losses reduce the owner’s equity as it also belongs
to the owners.
The (R – E) shows the relationship between the balance sheet and the income statement as
any profits or losses belong to the owners (owed to the owners).
A = L+OE is also presented by the balance sheet whilst (R-E) is presented by the income
statement and it has an effect on the balance sheet via OE.

Applying the double entry convention helps to maintain the equality of the accounting
equation. Equality of the accounting equation must be maintained at all times.

What is an account?

The basic recording unit. Each transaction is recorded into accounts. Accounts are the items
that are affected by the transaction. Accounts are the items that are affected by the
transaction and where transactions are subsequently recorded.

An account records any increase and decrease of assets, liabilities, revenue, expenses
and owner’s equity.

Accounts are found in the General Ledger. Accounts can be presented in either the T
format or the columnar format. As part of this course both formats will be discovered
and assessed. The overall and end results will be the same.

T-account
Accounts in T format are similar to the capital letter T with the name of an account
shown at the top. The left hand side of the T account is called the debit side and includes
column headings of the date, particulars and debit amount. The right hand side of the
T account is called the credit side and also includes column headings of the date,
particulars and credit amount. The particulars can also be called the description column
and is used to record the name of the corresponding account affected.

Chart of accounts

As there can be a large number of accounts in a business, a chart of accounts is
necessary and prepared to list all the accounts in the general ledger under the 5
groups/elements. Each account listed in the chart of accounts is allocated an account
code. This is necessary for easy identification and sorting of accounts. The account
code may be in a numeric or alpha-numeric sequence. The chart of accounts is
prepared at the commencement of the business and added to as the need arises in the
future.

Businesses in different industries will tend to have different accounts and therefore a
different chart of accounts. A chart of accounts is tailored to each individual business.
The common elements or groupings will remain the same.

Double entry explained

Under the universally used double-entry system, the dual (two-sided) effect of each
transaction is recorded in appropriate accounts. This system provides a logical method for
recording transactions. The double-entry system also offers a means of ensuring the accuracy
of the recorded amounts. If every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits, then
the sum of all the debits to the accounts must equal the sum of all the credits. The double-
entry system for determining the equality of the accounting equation is much more efficient
than the plus/minus procedure used earlier. Therefore, it was necessary after each transaction
to compare total assets with total liabilities and equity to determine the equality of the two
sides of the accounting equation.

Analyze transactions and prepare an analysis table

Recording transactions in the accounts requires following all the conventions and
concepts especially the double entry rule. The double entry rule requires that each
transaction affect at least two accounts; one on the debit side and the other on the
credit side. To decide which account has to be debited and which has to be credited,
each transaction has to be analysed. A table is drawn up to help analyse the transactions.
It is called an analysis chart.

Using the accounting equation as a guide Debits (Dr) and Credits (Cr) are used in
business to help explain associated increases and/or decreases of assets, liabilities,
revenue, expenses and owner’s equity.

Rules that should be followed are:
•
Record on the debit side if assets and expenses  increase.
•
Record on the credit side if assets and expenses decrease.
•
Record on the credit side if liabilities, revenues and
owner’s equity increase.
•
Record on the debit side if liabilities, revenues and owner’s
equity decrease.

To analyse transactions:

Follow the steps below to analyse a transaction.
1. Read the transaction description and identify the key words.
2. Determine the 2 accounts affected as a minimum (ie is it the cash at
bank account, capital account, furniture or loan account).
3. Determine the element or group of accounts for each account affected (ie A,
L, OE, R or E)
4. Determine the effect of the transaction on the accounting
equation (ie increase the assets or decrease the assets or
increase the owner’s equity or decrease the owner’s equity).
5. Apply the double entry rule to decide if each account should be
recorded on the debit side a n d / or credit side.

Note: The 2 important accounts
Accounts payable
Accounts payable is also known as creditors and is an example of current liabilities.
Accounts payable are businesses or persons from whom the business purchases trading
goods on credit and thus owes amounts.

E.g. Kingsford Trading bought toys from Queen Limited for $400, to be paid in
21 days.

Queen Limited is an Accounts payable for Kingsford Trading as they owe Queen
Limited money in return for goods purchased on credit.


Accounts receivable
Accounts receivable is also known as debtors and is an example of current assets.
Accounts receivable are businesses or persons that the business sells goods to on
credit.
 E.g. Kingsford Trading sold toys to Princess Limited for $500, to be paid in 14
days.

Princess Limited is an Accounts Receivable for Kingsford Trading as they will
receive money from Princess Limited for toys sold on credit.

Note: The 2 methods of recording inventory transactions

1. Perpetual – this method involves the business keeping track of each inventory item
and purchases of inventory are recorded in the Inventory account. Once a sale of
goods occurs the original cost is transferred out of the inventory account and into
the expense account named Cost Of Goods Sold (COGS). This method allows the
business to calculate the Gross Profit (ie Sales – COGS) immediately. This method
usually suits businesses where the tracking of individual inventory items is feasible
and cost effective. This course will not use this method.

2. Periodic – this method involves the business recording the purchase of Inventory
into the expense account named Purchases. Therefore once a sale of goods occurs
no transaction is required to the Inventory account at that point in time. However,
at the end of the accounting period a stocktake is undertaken (a physical counting
of Inventory on hand/not sold) and this is used together with the Inventory at the
beginning of the accounting period to determine the Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
and hence the Gross Profit. This method usually suits businesses where the
tracking of individual inventory items is not feasible and too costly. This course
will use this method only.

What is Accounting?
Accounting is an information process, i.e. a method of processing financial information
for different users. Accounting is often been called ‘the language of business’ as it is the
means of communicating financial information. It provides a way of recording, measuring
and describing economic activities.


The accounting function involves identifying, gathering, classifying, recording,
summarising, analysing, reporting and communicating financial information. Accounting
is practical tool used by many in varying jobs. People running a business need an
understanding of accounting to help them make sound decisions about their business.
The Role of an Accountant
As the commercial world has grown in size and complexity, the importance of the
accountant has also increased. The accountant's role has changed as the number of
interested parties has grown. The accountant must prepare information designed to
meet a wide variety of needs and expectations and is responsible to the users of that
information. The accountant's role is dynamic, important and varied. The principal role of
an accountant is to report on the performance of a business. This can involve tasks such
as:
•
Designing, implementing and managing systems for accurately recording and
classifying transactions and other data.
•
Using information obtained to prepare accounting reports which show the
financial performance and position of a business.
•
Communicating the information to all interested parties in a form which the users
can understand.

Areas of Accounting
Financial accountants prepare year-end financial statements as well as analyse and
interpret financial information. Managerial accountants produce, analyse and coordinate
the flow of information which will help in running the business e.g. budgeting, pricing
and planning for future needs. Cost accountants control the costs of producing goods
and services.

Auditors check the financial statements to ensure that the financial statements show a
'true and fair view' about the financial affairs of the business. They ensure that the
financial reports comply with regulations. Large companies employ independent auditors
to conduct audits in order to protect the interests of shareholders. Accountants may also
specialise in taxation, investment, estate control, banking or teaching.

Book-keeping Vs Accounting

Bookkeeping is the foundational process of recording financial transactions. It involves:
•
Data entry: Recording sales, purchases, and payments accurately and consistently.
•
Categorization: Sorting transactions into appropriate accounts accordingly.
•
Reconciliation: Matching

•
Financial reports: Generating basic financial statements like income statements
and balance sheets, providing a snapshot of the company’s financial health at a
specific time.
Bookkeepers are the meticulous record-keepers who ensure financial data is accurate, up-
to-date, and organized. Their work is essential for maintaining the integrity of a company’s
financial records and providing the raw data needed for accounting analysis.

Accountants take the raw data bookkeepers produce and transform it into meaningful
information. Their role involves:
•
Financial analysis: Examining a company’s financial data can reveal its financial
health, growth patterns, and competitive position. This involves calculating financial
ratios, tracking performance over time, and comparing results to industry
benchmarks.
•
Tax preparation: Calculating and filing tax returns accurately and on time, ensuring
compliance with tax laws and regulations.
•
Financial planning: Developing strategies to optimize financial resources and
improve overall performance. This includes creating and managing budgets, ensuring
efficient cash flow, and making sound investment decisions.
•
Compliance: Upholding accounting standards and regulations to ensure financial
integrity and transparency.
•
Auditing: Reviewing financial records to ensure accuracy and compliance, providing
assurance to stakeholders.

Some users of accounting information
•
Owners/ shareholders/ managers use the accounting information to assess the
financial position and performance and make informed decisions accordingly.
•
Investors use the accounting information to assess investment potential.
•
Lenders/ creditors/ banks use the accounting information to assess whether the
line of credit is sound.
•
Auditors use the accounting information to ensure internal controls are working
effectively and to prepare audit reports.
•
Tax authorities use the accounting information to assess tax liabilities.
•
Competitors use the accounting information to assess their own performance
against that of the business in the same Industry.

The essential requirements of accounting reports if they are to be useful for the
users are:
Clarity (understandability):
The reports are presented in such a way that reasonably-informed users can
comprehend their meaning, e.g. the use of headings and sub-headings may help to
highlight important information in a Profit and Loss Statement.
Accuracy (reliability):
The information should correspond with the actual underlying transactions and events,
and is capable of independent verification and is free from bias. The reports can be used
to confirm or correct expectations about past events and assist in forming, revising or
confirming expectations about the future.
Consistency (comparability):
Users must be able to identify similarities and differences between the current
information and information from previous years' reports or reports of different entities.
Timely
To be useful, the information prepared by accountants must also be timely i.e. reports
should be prepared in plenty of time for the people requiring the information to make
decisions. If information is not available when it is needed, it is of no use.
Compliance
Reports should comply with the requirements of accounting regulations and accounting
standards.

The Accounting Cycle
Overall, this Introduction to Accounting course will follow the accounting cycle through
each stage as a reference point with additional accounting concepts and fundamentals
added. Note also that this course refers to a manual accounting system as a
computerised accounting system automates some of the stages.
The accounting cycle refers to the regular repetition of a standardised set of stages
commencing once transactions occur and capturing the financial information in source

The Accounting Cycle steps
The accounting cycle is a continual process because business activity is ongoing. The
source documents, journals, ledgers, financial reports and subsequent analysis are then
carefully stored in a safe place and/or archived as they may be required for future
reference, comparisons and/or taxation purposes.
Stage 1 - Transactions:
Transactions are events that alter the financial position of a business and require the
entering of information into accounting records. Generally, this involves activities of
buying, selling and/or exchanging of goods and services for cash or on credit.
Stage 2 - Source Documents
Source Documents are the original financial documents that contains evidence and/or
proof of transactions occurring. The transaction information and details are collected in
the form of source documents. Some commonly used source documents include;
invoices, cheques, receipts, credit notes, bank deposit slips and bank statements. These
source documents contain the details that will then be recorded in the journals.
Stage 3 - Journals
Journals are the first book of entry that are used to record details and information from
source documents.
Stage 4 - Ledgers
The ledger is a book of secondary entry and contains all the accounts of a business.
Information from the journals are posted (or transferred) to the corresponding accounts
in the ledger.
Ledgers include the General Ledger where values from the journals are entered and
Accounts Payable and Receivable Subsidiary Ledgers individually.
The accounts in the ledger can be presented in either T format or columnar format. The
accounts are also classified and grouped into five elements. These include the Assets,
Liabilities, Owner’s Equity, Revenue and Expenses of a business (refer to Lecture 3).
Stage 5 - Trial balance
The Trial Balance is a list of all the account balances in the General Ledger (after the
balancing process for each account is completed). Debit balances are listed in the debit
column of the Trial Balance and credit balances are listed in the credit column. The totals
for the debit and credit columns are calculated to check if they are equal. This ensures
that the accounting rules have been followed (ie double entry rule) and the equality of
the accounting equation has been maintained.
Stage 6 - Reports
The trial balance is used to prepare the financial reports of a business. The reports are
prepared at the end of the accounting period. The 2 main reports for the purposes of this
course are the Profit and Loss Statement and the Balance Sheet.

The Profit and Loss Statement is also known as the Income Statement or the
Statement of Financial Performance. It contains information about the revenues (R) and
expenses (E) of a business for a defined accounting period, hence the profit or loss (R-E)
of a business and how the business has performed.
The Balance Sheet or the Statement of Financial Position displays the assets, liabilities
and owner’s equity of the business at a point in time.
The Cash Flow Statement is a financial report that tracks the movement of cash into and
out of a business over a specific period, detailing how much cash is generated and spent
from operating, investing, and financing activities. Unlike an income statement that includes
non-cash transactions, a cash flow statement focuses solely on actual cash movements to
provide insight into a company's liquidity, financial health, and ability to meet its financial
obligations
After the financial statements are prepared and presented they are then analysed in
order to assess the profitability, liquidity, solvency and management effectiveness of the
business. The financial reports and their subsequent analysis will be used by interested
parties such as owners, managers, creditors and investors for informed decision making.
The accounting cycle then begins for the next accounting period.
